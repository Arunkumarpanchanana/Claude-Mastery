Diligence & Responsible AI Use

Build the privacy-aware, accountability-conscious habits that make AI use genuinely responsible — in your work and life.

25 min Beginner Policy & Habits Lab 5 Quiz Questions

Privacy and Data in AI Interactions

When you use Claude, everything you type goes to Anthropic's servers. This is true for any cloud-based AI service. What you type in the chat box is not private in the way a conversation with a therapist or lawyer is. Anthropic has a privacy policy — but you should understand what it means in practice.

Data you should NOT share in Claude prompts:
  • Personally identifiable information about others (client names, SSNs, medical records)
  • Confidential business information (trade secrets, unreleased financials, M&A details)
  • Login credentials, API keys, passwords
  • Personal health or financial information you want kept private
  • Information under NDA or attorney-client privilege

The practical rule: before typing anything into Claude, ask yourself "Would I be comfortable if this appeared in a data breach or a training set?" If not, either anonymize it or don't include it.

Anonymization: Sharing Without Exposing

Most tasks that feel like they require private data can be accomplished with anonymized versions. Learning to anonymize well lets you use Claude effectively while protecting sensitive information.

Risky (real data)

Help me write a performance review for Sarah Johnson who works as a Senior Engineer at Acme Corp and makes $145,000. Her SSN is 123-45-6789 and she had a disciplinary incident in March.

Safe (anonymized)

Help me write a performance review for a Senior Engineer. Their achievements: [list]. Growth areas: [list]. There was one disciplinary incident to address constructively. Tone: professional and fair.

The anonymized version is actually a better prompt — it gives Claude what it needs (role, achievements, growth areas, tone) without exposing private data.

Human Oversight and Accountability

The most important principle of responsible AI use: the human is always accountable for the outcome. Claude is a tool. If a Claude-drafted email causes harm, you sent it. If Claude-generated code crashes a system, you deployed it. If Claude-written advice leads someone astray, you gave it.

This accountability principle shapes how much oversight you apply:

1

Low stakes: review once, use

A draft email to a colleague, a brainstormed list, an explanation for yourself. Read it, make sure it's appropriate, use it. The cost of error is low.

2

Medium stakes: review carefully, edit, use

A report for management, code that goes into a shared codebase, advice you'll share with others. Review thoroughly, edit substantially, fact-check key claims.

3

High stakes: expert review before use

Medical information you'll act on, legal documents, financial advice, safety-critical code. Have a qualified human expert review before any action. Claude helps you prepare; the expert validates.

Attribution and Intellectual Honesty

When AI substantially produces your work, there are emerging norms around attribution — and these norms differ by context:

Academic contexts

Most institutions have AI use policies. Submitting AI-generated work as your own is typically academic dishonesty. When in doubt, disclose. Some assignments permit AI assistance with attribution.

Professional contexts

Norms are still forming. Generally: using AI for first drafts is accepted; using AI to represent views you don't hold is problematic. Check your organization's policy.

Creative/publishing contexts

Many publishers require AI disclosure. Using AI as an editing tool is different from AI generating the work. Know the requirements of your platform.

The attribution rule of thumb: When AI substantially shaped the ideas, structure, or words — not just grammar — disclosure is generally appropriate. When AI was a tool (like spell-check) that you directed and substantially edited, attribution becomes less critical. When uncertain, err toward transparency.

Lab: Building Your Responsible AI Habits

Develop a personal AI use policy and practice the privacy-protective habits that make AI use sustainable and trustworthy.

1

Audit your current AI use for privacy risks

Think about the last 5-10 times you used Claude or another AI tool. For each, ask:

  • Did I include any real names of people who didn't consent?
  • Did I include any confidential company information?
  • Did I include any sensitive personal data?

If yes: how would you anonymize that input next time? Write down the anonymization version.

2

Practice anonymization

Take a real prompt you want to use that involves private data. Rewrite it with all sensitive information replaced by generic placeholders, then test it with Claude. Does it produce what you need without the sensitive data?

Original: "Review this email from my client John Smith at ABC Corp about their contract renewal..."

Anonymized: "Review this email from a client about a contract renewal. [paste email with names removed or replaced with [CLIENT] and [COMPANY]]"
3

Rate your recent AI tasks by stakes

List 5 things you've used Claude for recently. Rate each by stakes level:

  • Low stakes (error has minor consequences)
  • Medium stakes (error is embarrassing or wastes time)
  • High stakes (error has serious professional or personal consequences)

For your highest-stakes item: did you apply appropriate oversight? What oversight would be appropriate for that task going forward?

4

Write your personal AI use policy

Ask Claude to help you draft a 1-page personal AI use policy:

Help me write a short personal AI use policy for my work as a [your role]. It should cover:
1. What types of data I won't share with AI tools
2. What oversight I'll apply based on stakes level
3. How I'll handle attribution in my context
4. Any domain-specific considerations (e.g., if in healthcare, law, education)
Keep it practical and specific, not generic.

Then review and edit it. This document, once finalized, guides your AI use sustainably.

5

Check your organization's AI policy

Does your employer, school, or professional association have an AI use policy? If yes, read it. If no, should it? Note any areas where your personal policy and your organization's policy may need alignment.

Ask Claude: "What are the key elements that a good organizational AI use policy should cover?" Use this to evaluate or advocate for a better policy where you work.
