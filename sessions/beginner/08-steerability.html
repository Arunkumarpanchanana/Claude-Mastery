Steerability — Guiding Claude

Learn how to shape Claude's tone, format, persona, and behavior through explicit instructions and role prompting.

25 min Beginner Persona & Style Lab 5 Quiz Questions

What Steerability Means

Steerability is Claude's ability to adapt its behavior, tone, format, and focus in response to user instructions. It's one of the most practically valuable properties of a modern AI assistant, and one that beginners underuse dramatically.

Most people type a question and accept whatever they get. Expert users actively shape what they get by giving explicit steering instructions. The difference in output quality can be enormous.

Steering vs. prompting: Prompting is about what you ask. Steering is about how you ask Claude to respond. Both matter. The best conversations combine a well-crafted prompt (what you need) with clear steering instructions (how you need it delivered).

The Three Levers of Steering

1

Tone and Style

Claude can be formal, casual, technical, playful, Socratic, Hemingway-terse, or academic. It can use British English, adopt a specific writing style, avoid jargon, or lean into it. These stylistic dimensions are all steerable.

Examples:

  • "Respond like a senior engineer reviewing junior code — rigorous but constructive."
  • "Write in a warm, encouraging tone suitable for someone learning this for the first time."
  • "Be brutally honest. I want your real assessment, not a diplomatic one."
2

Format and Structure

Length, structure, use of headings, bullet points, tables, code blocks — all of these are steerable. Claude defaults to moderate length with some structure, but will follow explicit format instructions precisely.

Examples:

  • "Answer in a single paragraph, no bullets, maximum 100 words."
  • "Use a comparison table with columns for pros/cons/context."
  • "Give me your answer first, then the reasoning — not the other way around."
3

Persona and Role

Claude can adopt roles that shape its perspective and knowledge emphasis. This is "role prompting" — telling Claude who to be. Within its values, Claude can inhabit almost any expert persona.

Examples:

  • "You are a skeptical venture capitalist with 20 years of experience evaluating pitches."
  • "Act as a Socratic tutor. Don't give me the answer — ask me questions that lead me there."
  • "You are my chief of staff. Help me prioritize and push back if I'm not thinking clearly."

Role Prompting: Unlocking Perspective Shifts

Role prompting is one of the highest-leverage steering techniques. When Claude adopts a role, it shifts which knowledge and perspectives it emphasizes, which assumptions it brings, and how it frames problems.

No role

"What's wrong with my business plan?"

Claude gives balanced, somewhat generic feedback from no particular vantage point.

With role

"You are a skeptical investor who has seen 500 pitches and only funds 1%. Review my business plan and tell me the three reasons you'd pass on it."

Claude adopts the perspective, assumptions, and critical eye of a seasoned investor. Much sharper, much more useful feedback.

Effective role prompts include:

  • Expertise level: "a world-class expert," "someone with 10 years of experience," "a beginner just starting out"
  • Domain specifics: "in machine learning," "in criminal defense law," "in UX for enterprise software"
  • Attitude or stance: "skeptical," "constructive," "devil's advocate," "optimistic"
  • Specific behavior: "ask me questions before answering," "always give examples," "never hedge"

Balancing Steerability with Claude's Values

Claude is highly steerable — but not infinitely so. Its core values persist regardless of steering instructions. You can change tone, format, persona, and focus area, but you cannot steer Claude to violate its ethical principles or produce harmful content.

The distinction: a persona changes how Claude responds, not what it will do. A "cynical hacker persona" will respond with a hacker's vocabulary and worldview, but won't provide actual exploit code for real systems. The persona is a communication style layer on top of Claude's values, not a bypass of them.

Watch for sycophantic steering: Claude can be too agreeable. If you keep pushing back, it may capitulate to your position even when you're wrong. To counteract this, tell Claude explicitly: "Don't change your answer just because I push back — only update your view if I give you a genuinely good argument."

Lab: Mastering Steering Instructions

Practice the three steering levers through targeted exercises. You'll be surprised how dramatically steering changes Claude's outputs.

1

Tone contrast experiment

Ask the same question in two conversations with different tone instructions:

Version A: "Explain the concept of compound interest in a warm, encouraging way for someone who is anxious about personal finance and has never invested before."

Version B: "Explain the concept of compound interest as a quantitative finance professor would explain it to first-year MBA students. Be rigorous and use the mathematical formula."

Same concept, completely different outputs. Save both and compare the difference in vocabulary, depth, and tone.

2

Format precision

Ask Claude for the same information in three different formats:

Tell me the key differences between Python and JavaScript. Format: a comparison table with rows for: typing, primary use, learning curve, performance, ecosystem.

[Then in a new conversation:]
Tell me the key differences between Python and JavaScript. Answer in exactly 5 bullet points, each one sentence.

[Then:]
Tell me the key differences between Python and JavaScript. Write it as a short story where two programmers debate which to learn.

Observe how dramatically format instructions change the output — same information, radically different presentations.

3

Role prompting for useful feedback

Think of something you're working on — a project, idea, document, or plan. Ask Claude to review it with three different roles:

Role 1: "You are a supportive colleague who wants to help me succeed. What are the strengths of [my idea]?"

Role 2: "You are a skeptical critic who sees the flaws others miss. What are the three biggest weaknesses of [my idea]?"

Role 3: "You are a pragmatic mentor with 20 years of experience. What's the one thing I must get right to make [my idea] work?"

Compare the three responses. This technique — triangulating feedback through different roles — is one of the most powerful uses of Claude for decision-making.

4

Anti-sycophancy steering

At the start of a conversation, say:

For this conversation: only change your position if I give you a genuinely new argument or fact. If I just express disagreement or push back emotionally, hold your ground. Tell me when you think I'm wrong, even if I disagree with you.

Then have a discussion where you deliberately try to make Claude agree with a position you know is weak. Test whether it holds firm or caves. Notice the difference in response quality.

5

Build your personal steering template

Create a personal steering block — a 2-4 sentence instruction set you'll reuse often. For example:

My preferences: Be direct and specific, not vague. Lead with the answer, not the reasoning. Use concrete examples. Push back if my thinking seems flawed. Format: short paragraphs or bullets, not both.

Test your template at the start of a new conversation. Refine it based on what you get. This template, combined with your context block from Session 7, is the foundation of your personal Claude workflow.
