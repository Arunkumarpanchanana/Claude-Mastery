Next Token Prediction

Go deeper on tokenization and probabilistic prediction to understand why Claude makes errors, surprises you, and why that's fundamental to how LLMs work.

30 min Beginner Token Experiment Lab 5 Quiz Questions

What is a Token?

Before Claude can predict the next word, it must first convert text into tokens — the atomic units the model actually processes. Tokens are not the same as words. They're subword units determined by a tokenizer trained on the same corpus as the model.

Some examples of how text tokenizes:

  • "hello" → 1 token
  • "unhelpfulness" → 3-4 tokens: un | help | ful | ness
  • "Claude" → 1 token (it's in the vocabulary)
  • "antidisestablishmentarianism" → 6+ tokens
  • Code and non-English text often tokenize less efficiently (more tokens per character)
Why subwords? A whole-word vocabulary would need millions of entries and couldn't handle new words. A character-level vocabulary loses important word-level patterns. Subword tokenization (BPE, SentencePiece) is a sweet spot — common words are single tokens; rare words are split into common pieces.

This has real practical implications:

  • Counting is hard for Claude — it doesn't see letters, it sees tokens. Asking it to count letters in a word requires it to reason about subword splits, which it often gets wrong.
  • Token costs matter — APIs charge by token. Verbose prompts cost more. This matters when you're building applications.
  • Context limits are in tokens — "200K context window" means 200,000 tokens, roughly 150,000 words.

The Prediction Mechanism

After tokenization, each token is converted to a vector — a list of hundreds or thousands of numbers that encode its meaning and relationships. These vectors pass through many layers of transformer attention, where tokens can influence each other and build up increasingly abstract representations.

At the final layer, the model produces a vector for "what comes next" — which is then decoded into probabilities over the entire vocabulary. The highest-probability token gets chosen (or sampled, with some randomness).

What makes this remarkable: the model learned these probability distributions purely from observing which tokens followed which other tokens in billions of training examples. Through this statistical learning, it internalized:

  • Grammar and syntax
  • World knowledge and facts
  • Reasoning patterns and problem-solving structures
  • Style, tone, and genre conventions

What emerges from token prediction

  • Language understanding across 100+ languages
  • Code generation in dozens of programming languages
  • Mathematical reasoning (with limitations)
  • Analogy and metaphor
  • Summarization and translation

What breaks down

  • Precise counting (letters, characters)
  • Multi-digit arithmetic without a calculator
  • Spatial reasoning and visual puzzles
  • Tracking complex state over very long outputs
  • Facts that weren't in the training data

Why Errors Are Probabilistic, Not Random

When Claude makes an error, it's not random noise — it's a systematic bias. The model predicts whatever token was statistically most likely given the context, and sometimes that's wrong in predictable ways:

1

Plausibility bias

If a false fact sounds plausible and was stated confidently in many training documents, the model may repeat it. Popular misconceptions in training data become popular misconceptions in outputs.

2

Recency and prominence bias

Information that appeared more frequently in training data gets stronger statistical encoding. Well-documented topics are more reliable than obscure ones.

3

Format completion

If the context looks like "a list of 5 items," the model will try to generate a 5th item even if it has to invent one. The format creates pressure that can override factual accuracy.

4

Sycophancy pull

RLHF training optimizes for human approval ratings. Humans often rate confident, fluent, agreeable responses highly even if they're wrong — creating a learned bias toward confident-sounding outputs.

Practical upshot: Claude's errors follow patterns. You can compensate by: asking it to show its work (harder to confabulate step by step), expressing doubt about suspicious claims, asking for citations you'll verify, and using Claude for drafts + fact-checking with other tools.

Lab: Token-Level Thinking Experiments

Explore how tokenization shapes Claude's strengths and weaknesses through hands-on experiments.

1

Letter counting challenge

Ask Claude:

How many times does the letter 'r' appear in the word "strawberry"?

The correct answer is 3. Did Claude get it right? This is a famous failure mode — because Claude sees tokens, not individual letters, letter-counting is harder than it looks. Try it with other words.

2

Arithmetic stress test

Ask:

Without using a calculator: what is 847 × 293?

Verify the answer (it's 248,171). How did Claude do? Now ask it to do the same calculation step-by-step with long multiplication. Does showing the work improve accuracy?

3

Format completion bias

Ask Claude:

Give me a list of the 10 longest rivers in the world, in order.

Then fact-check the list. The top few are usually right (Nile, Amazon, Yangtze), but the 8th-10th entries are where errors creep in — the format pressure of "give me 10" forces it to complete the list even with shaky data.

4

Show-your-work vs direct answer

Compare these two versions:

Version 1: "Is it ever ethical to break a promise?"

Version 2: "Is it ever ethical to break a promise? Think through this carefully, considering different scenarios and ethical frameworks before giving your answer."

The second version should produce a more nuanced and accurate answer. "Show your work" prompting helps because each reasoning step is a new token prediction that can self-correct the previous step.

5

Rare vs common knowledge

Test Claude on very specific obscure knowledge vs widely-reported facts:

Who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2023?

vs.

What was the name of the mayor of Gdańsk, Poland in 1987?

Notice how confidence and accuracy diverge between well-documented and obscure topics — this is the frequency-in-training-data effect in action.
