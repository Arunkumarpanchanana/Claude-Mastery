Delegation — Handing Tasks to Claude

Master the art of knowing what to delegate to Claude, how to decompose complex projects, and how to build a reliable delegation-diligence loop.

30 min Beginner Task Delegation Lab 5 Quiz Questions

What Makes a Good Delegation Candidate?

Not every task benefits from AI delegation. The best delegation candidates share certain characteristics — understanding these prevents wasted effort and over-reliance on AI where human judgment is irreplaceable.

Strong Delegation Candidates

  • Information-dense drafting — first drafts of documents, emails, reports
  • Pattern recognition at scale — analyzing many items, spotting common themes
  • Structured thinking tasks — frameworks, outlines, checklists, plans
  • Expertise translation — turning complex content into accessible explanations
  • Research synthesis — combining information from multiple inputs you provide
  • Repetitive writing — templates, standardized responses, similar documents

Keep Human-Driven

  • Decisions with accountability — hiring, firing, large purchases
  • Relationship management — difficult conversations, negotiations
  • Novel judgment calls — unprecedented situations with no clear right answer
  • Creative vision — the artistic or strategic choices, not the execution
  • Ethical determinations — what's right vs. wrong in complex situations
  • Tasks with real-time data — anything needing current information
The delegation spectrum: Most tasks aren't fully delegate-able OR fully human-only. They're mixtures. The skill is identifying which components of a task are good delegation candidates and which components need human judgment — then structuring the work accordingly.

Task Decomposition for AI Delegation

Complex projects can feel impossible to delegate to Claude — until you break them down. Task decomposition is the process of splitting a large, complex goal into smaller components, each of which can be handled more effectively by Claude or by you.

1

Identify the end goal

What does "done" look like? Be specific. "Write a business plan" is too vague. "Produce a 10-page plan including executive summary, market analysis, financial projections, and go-to-market strategy" is concrete.

2

List the components

Break the goal into distinct deliverables or phases. Ask Claude to help: "What are the main components of a business plan?" Now you have a list of smaller tasks.

3

Classify each component

For each piece: Is this execution (Claude can draft) or judgment (I decide)? The market analysis section is mostly execution — Claude can draft it from information you provide. The strategic direction is judgment — you decide, Claude helps you articulate it.

4

Sequence and batch

Some components depend on others. Draft the executive summary last, after the other sections exist. Group related sub-tasks. This gives you a roadmap for using Claude across many sessions.

Project Planning with Claude

Claude is excellent at helping you plan projects — not by making the decisions, but by scaffolding your thinking. Use Claude as a thinking partner at the project level, then as an executor at the task level.

The planning prompt pattern:

I'm planning to [project description]. My goal is [outcome]. My constraints are [time/resources/skills].

Step 1: Help me identify everything that needs to happen to achieve this.
Step 2: Identify the critical path — what must happen before what?
Step 3: Flag where I'll need specialized help or skills I don't have.
Step 4: Suggest what you can help me with vs. what I need to do myself.

This pattern leverages Claude's strength at structured thinking while keeping you in control of the actual decisions.

The Delegation-Diligence Loop

Delegation doesn't end when Claude produces output. It closes when you apply Diligence — reviewing, verifying, and integrating that output responsibly. The loop:

→

Delegate → Review → Integrate → Iterate

You delegate a task, Claude produces output, you review it (Discernment), integrate it into your work (with modifications), and delegate the next task. Good delegation is iterative, not one-shot.

The time math: If Claude saves you 2 hours of drafting but you spend 30 minutes reviewing and editing — you're still ahead by 90 minutes and you end up with a better output than Claude's first draft. Good delegation is about net time savings and quality outcomes, not zero-effort.

Lab: Delegate a Real Project

Work through a real project or goal from your life using task decomposition and strategic delegation.

1

Choose a real goal

Pick a project you actually need to do in the next month. It could be professional (launch a new process, write a proposal), personal (plan a trip, learn a skill, start a side project), or educational (complete a course, write a paper).

Write a one-sentence description of the goal and the outcome you want.

2

Get Claude to decompose it

Ask Claude:

I'm working toward this goal: [your goal]. My deadline is [date/timeframe] and my main constraints are [time, money, skills, etc.].

Please:
1. List every component or milestone needed to achieve this
2. Identify the critical path (what depends on what)
3. Tell me which parts you can help me with vs. which require my own judgment or action

Review what Claude produces. Is anything missing? Is the critical path correct?

3

Classify the components

Take Claude's component list and classify each item:

  • Green: Claude can do this (drafting, researching, structuring)
  • Yellow: Claude can help but I make the final call (analysis, evaluation)
  • Red: This is mine alone (decisions, relationships, accountability)

What percentage of your project is in each category? Most knowledge work projects are 50-70% green or yellow.

4

Execute one delegation

Pick a green-category task from your list and delegate it to Claude right now. Write a full prompt using Description skills (context, task, format, constraints). Then evaluate the output using Discernment: what's good, what needs work, what will you change?

5

Reflect on the delegation experience

Write down:

  • How much time did Claude's help save?
  • What would this have looked like if you'd done it alone?
  • What were the limitations of the delegation?
  • What would you do differently next time?

This reflection builds deliberate delegation skill much faster than just using Claude habitually.
