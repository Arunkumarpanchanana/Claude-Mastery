Anatomy of a Good Prompt

Every message you send Claude is a prompt. The quality of Claude's response is heavily shaped by the quality of your prompt. But "quality" doesn't mean length — a three-word prompt can be excellent, and a ten-paragraph prompt can be terrible. What matters is clarity of intent.

A well-structured prompt typically answers these questions, explicitly or implicitly:

1

What do you want? (Task)

The core request. "Write," "Explain," "Compare," "Summarize," "Debug," "Brainstorm" — start with an action verb. Vague requests like "tell me about X" produce vague responses.

2

Why do you want it? (Context)

Context transforms generic answers into tailored ones. "Explain photosynthesis" gets a textbook answer. "Explain photosynthesis for my 8-year-old who loves Minecraft" gets something actually useful.

3

What form should it take? (Format)

If you need bullet points, say so. If you need a 200-word summary, specify it. If you want code with comments, ask for it. Claude will guess at format, but your guess is usually better than Claude's guess.

4

What are the constraints? (Limits)

What should Claude avoid? What assumptions shouldn't it make? Constraints reduce the search space and help Claude land closer to what you actually need.

Weak Prompt

"Tell me about project management"

Claude has to guess: what level? what context? what aspect? The response will be generic.

Strong Prompt

"I'm a software engineer moving into a team lead role. Summarize the 3 most important project management concepts I need to understand in my first 90 days. Use bullet points, keep it practical."

Clear task, specific context, explicit format, implicit constraints (practical, not theoretical).

Tone Matching — Getting the Right Register

Claude is remarkably adaptable in tone and register. It will match the formality level of your message, mirror technical vocabulary back to you, and adjust depth based on cues in your language. You can — and should — make this explicit.

Tone vocabulary to use with Claude: casual, formal, technical, simple, conversational, Socratic, terse, thorough, friendly, direct. You can combine these: "Be direct and technical, no fluff."

Tone instructions at the start of a conversation persist. If you open with "Please be concise and don't use bullet points," Claude will honor that throughout — until you change it. This is a powerful tool for customizing your working style.

Some useful tone settings:

  • For brainstorming: "Think out loud, share half-formed ideas, don't polish."
  • For technical work: "Assume I have a CS background. Skip basic explanations."
  • For sensitive topics: "Be direct and honest, even if the truth is uncomfortable."
  • For learning: "Use analogies and examples. Check if I understand before moving on."

Iteration — The Real Skill

New users treat Claude like a search engine: one query, one answer, done. Expert users treat Claude like a conversation: they ask, evaluate, refine, push back, and steer. The best outputs almost always come from iteration, not from a perfect first prompt.

Here's what productive iteration looks like:

A

Ask for alternatives

"Give me 3 different approaches to this." Then pick the best and refine it.

B

Add what's missing

"Good, but now make it shorter / more technical / focused on the cost angle."

C

Challenge the output

"I'm not convinced by point 2. What's the counter-argument?" This forces Claude to think more carefully.

D

Ask follow-up questions

"You mentioned X — can you explain that in more detail?" Conversations compound; each turn can build on what came before.

Pro move: After Claude gives an answer you like, ask "What important considerations did you not mention?" This surfaces blind spots and often yields the most valuable information.

Lab: Prompt Iteration in Practice

This lab walks you through a realistic prompt improvement exercise. You'll take a weak prompt, improve it step by step, and see the difference in output quality.

1

Start with a weak prompt

In a new Claude conversation, type:

Help me write an email

Observe: Claude will ask clarifying questions or make assumptions. Note what it needs to know that you didn't provide.

2

Add context and constraints

Now try this improved version (adapt to your own situation):

Write a professional email to my manager requesting one day of work-from-home per week. I've been at the company 18 months, my role is mostly independent work (no client calls), and my team already has two people who WFH Fridays. Keep it under 150 words, confident but not demanding in tone.

Compare the outputs. The second prompt specifies task, context, relevant facts, format, and tone. Notice how much more targeted the result is.

3

Iterate on the result

After getting the email in step 2, do at least two of these follow-ups:

Make it warmer and more personal — right now it sounds too corporate.

Also add a brief mention that I'm happy to discuss any concerns.

Then try: Give me an alternative version that's even shorter — just 3 sentences.

4

Use the "what's missing" technique

After you have a version you like, ask:

What important considerations about requesting remote work did you not include in this email? Should any of them be added?

This often surfaces things like: proposing a trial period, noting productivity metrics, or mentioning communication plans — considerations you might not have thought of.

5

Reflect on the process

How many iterations did it take to get something genuinely useful? Most people find that 2-4 turns produces something much better than the first response. This is normal and expected — iteration is the skill, not perfect first prompts.
